Sri Lanka is a country full of contrasts – jungle, tea plantations, temples and
dream beaches all packed into a small space. Laura and I spent two weeks as
travel buddies crossing the island: from the mountains to the south coast.
Here's our travel diary, day by day, in our own words.

It started with a long travel day. Well fuelled up after a breakfast buffet,
we boarded the plane with a 9.2 kg and a 10 kg backpack (plus a decent amount
of hand luggage). After almost six hours of flying we landed for a
three-hour layover in Doha, then continued another four hours to Colombo. On
the last flight, Sulakkya sat next to us, who already had 14 hours of travel
from Texas behind him – so we didn't feel like complaining. Since he was
originally from Sri Lanka, he gave us our first tips right there, like which
drinks to try against a hangover. Luckily everything worked out with the
backpacks, we got SIM cards, and a driver from the hotel picked us up
directly. At around 5 a.m. local time, after 18 hours of travelling, we fell
into bed.

## Sigiriya – a hotel in the middle of the jungle

Day two was immediately another travel day. We slept in until 11, checked
out, enjoyed a tasty lunch, and then spent 3.5 hours travelling to Sigiriya.
Even though our driver's English was patchy, the ride was pretty
entertaining – with a few monkey stops thrown in, the time flew by.

Somehow the new hotel in the middle of the jungle didn't sit right with us.
The prospect of a wild elephant possibly standing in the driveway in the dark
that evening didn't exactly reassure us. So we walked to the main road and
enjoyed bad cocktails and a bad burger, then switched bars for a beer. Even
though the beer was warm and only bearable with a few (forbidden) ice cubes,
we started fine-tuning our travel plans – and felt optimistic about the days
ahead.

The next morning the alarm went off at 5:00 a.m. after a few unsettling
noises in the night. ⏰ It was dark outside and definitely too creepy, so we
deliberately dawdled and waited until the sun had come up a bit – so that, if
worst came to worst, we could face the elephants in daylight. While we
waited for a tuk-tuk, I tried a honey hopper for breakfast. Five minutes
later we reached **Pidurangala Rock**. First we passed the sleeping Buddha –
even with your eyes closed, knees and shoulders weren't allowed to show.
After that came a short but strenuous climb, which even involved a bit of
actual scrambling near the top. Since most people had already been up there
for sunrise, our lateness worked in our favour: we practically had the
viewing platform overlooking **Lion Rock** to ourselves.

## Kandy – the Temple of the Tooth and locked doors

After a small breakfast and a drive of just over two hours that felt
noticeably longer, we arrived in Kandy. We'd traded jungle noises for loud
car horns – good thing I'd brought earplugs. The hotel, at least, felt
friendlier, brighter and more central. Fuelled by a 70-cent meal, we visited
the **Temple of the Tooth**, said to house Buddha's left canine tooth. It
wasn't exactly impressive, but we left a few flowers too – who knows what
that karma might be good for.

At the big market we got annoyed pretty quickly, because everything was
packed and everyone was relentlessly trying to talk us into stopping or
taking a tuk-tuk somewhere. At a rooftop restaurant, though, we found a pizza
that cost three times as much as everything else here – but the price and
the view were worth it. After that, a short stop at a bar playing what was
basically elevator music, where once again someone struck up a seemingly
friendly conversation only to try to sell us a tour at the end. To our shock,
we then discovered the town was like a ghost town and our hotel was locked
up. Nobody had told us we wouldn't be able to get in after 10:30 p.m. After
calling several times, someone let us in – an experience we didn't need a
second time.

The next day was calmer. After a typically Sri Lankan breakfast – roti with
curry – we took a tuk-tuk for about an hour to the **Ambuluwawa Tower**, a
striking tower that combines architectural elements from various religions.
Sadly, the weather wasn't on our side: we arrived in a heavy thunderstorm.
The view was better than expected, but we didn't dare go all the way to the
top, since the tower felt fairly unstable and everything was slippery, wet
and windy. On the way back we made a 90-minute stop at the **Botanical
Garden**, which we had almost entirely to ourselves – the longer we looked
into the trees, the more flying foxes we spotted. In the evening we finally
tried **kottu roti**: the roti is cut into strips and fried up with spicy
seasoning and vegetables – a delicious change from all the rice.

## By train to Ella

The alarm went off at 7:00 a.m. for the legendary **train ride from Kandy to
Ella**. Tickets online had been sold out for weeks, and there's no real
timetable to speak of anyway. Our luck: being pretty much first at the
station, someone had just cancelled a ticket for four people – only 3rd
class, but we took it anyway and sold the two spare seats to two girls from
Mainz. In the standing compartment next door there wasn't even room to turn
around, whereas we had reasonably comfortable seats by the window.

The real highlight: the open doors during the ride and the view over
countless tea plantations. But after five hours at the latest, even the most
beautiful view starts to wear you out. After a total of 7.5 hours on the
train (basically the equivalent of Cologne to Sylt), we had hopefully
covered the longest stretch of the whole trip – half that train experience
would have done the job too. Ella, on the other hand, was a pleasant
surprise right away: finally a few bars and pubs. We headed straight to the
Wood House, where we felt right at home thanks to the amazing view, and then,
starving, into town. The kottu tasted especially good, and to celebrate the
day there was cold draught beer. Good night from the canopy bed.

## Ella – waterfall, tea plantation and cooking class

After a delicious breakfast with a dreamy view, our host organised a driver
for the day – today's upgrade: a Bluetooth speaker, which at the driver's
request gave us a little Peter Fox sample. After a 20-minute climb we reached
a stunning **waterfall**, almost entirely alone. In the bright sunshine the
cool water felt great, even if a few nippy fish in the pool triggered the
occasional spontaneous leap to the side.

Next up was Ella's biggest **tea factory**. Since we only had an hour left
before the rain, we did the tea plantation tour first – smart move: we not
only paid the entrance fee, we also helped with the harvest. Our
hand-picked tea is export-ready within 38 hours and might already be sitting
in someone's cup somewhere next week. After a tour through the tea
production process there was a tasting, and then, timed just right, we
headed back in pouring rain. After a short break, a **cooking class**
followed around 5 p.m. – more work, this time for our own dinner. But the
work paid off: various curries and homemade bread. We wrapped up the evening
with beer and good music.

## A safari letdown and the drive to the coast

The next morning brought the last long journey. After breakfast with a
dreamy view, we checked out the **Nine Arches Bridge** – in our opinion,
completely overrated, packed with tourists, and overlooking a construction
site. Even on the way there, a few elephants blocked our path, an experience
that visibly rattled even our driver.

The booked **safari** turned out to be simply disappointing: four hours of
being jolted over the worst potholes imaginable, and apart from a sore neck
there wasn't much to take away. After 3.5 hours of torture we finally saw
elephants, but were so exhausted that we were mostly just relieved to get
back to the parking lot. For us, that's worth a bad Google review at best.
To top it off, there was bad food and a two-hour drive to **Hiriketiya**.
Finally at the coast – the beds were cosy, and thankfully our first jungle
hotel seemed to have been a one-off.

## Hiriketiya – surfing and relaxing

The next day was right up my alley. While Laura made up for the lack of
sleep from the last few days, I was on the beach before 9 a.m., grabbing the
first board before all the surf lessons started. Even though my first surf
lesson was a while back, I was standing pretty confidently before long – and
concluded that I'm actually reasonably decent at this. By midday it was far
too crowded to find a spot without a surf instructor, and despite Laura's
lively impression of our last surf instructor, Alfonso, there were barely
any waves, just a sunburn.

The next day, to my delight, I found T-shirts on the beach again – the
longest one at least covered a good chunk of the sunburn, though it wasn't
enough to save the backs of my knees. With a little help from Laura and her
impression of a professional surf instructor, we managed to ride a few waves
all the way to the beach. Late in the afternoon there was a bodysurfing duel
against a Spanish dad – no chance of winning, but it earned an appreciative
"good performance." At least I beat Laura 10:7. Nightlife here, too, was
pretty sparse, even on a Saturday.

## Weligama and Mirissa

In the morning we drove about ten minutes to **Turtle Point**, a small, cute
beach – though apart from one turtle there wasn't much to see. Once it got
too crowded, we headed over to **Weligama**, to the nicest hotel of the whole
trip. For the equivalent of just under three euros, you can rent a surfboard
there and get a sun lounger thrown in for free. The waves are super
beginner-friendly, so even Laura, with her injured knee, dared to catch a
wave or two – and with only a few other surfers in the water, it was much
more fun.

The next day started off solo for me, since the bolognese hadn't agreed with
Laura. For the equivalent of about 0.16 cents I took the bus to **Mirissa** –
riding the bus here is a real experience: the bus always has right of way,
the horn only sounds once, and if you're not fast enough getting on or off,
it simply drives on. At **Parrot Rock**, which you can only reach at low
tide, only crows were still around, and apart from a few fish there wasn't
much to snorkel at Turtle Point either. Still, a lovely beach for reading. On
the way back I hunted down bananas and milk rolls for Laura and picked up
some medicine at the pharmacy.

Thankfully Laura felt better the next morning. While she worked up the
courage for a milk roll, I treated myself to a full-body massage for under
€13. We took the bus to a **turtle rescue and conservation station**, where
we learned a lot about the small and injured animals there. Afterwards, for
a donation, we got to release two baby turtles into the sea ourselves – good
for karma, for a long life and fertility, and we also got to make a wish.
I'm fairly sure little Franklin is now living happily in the ocean for the
next 150 years. After a visit to a small bay in Anagahma, we walked along
the beach at sunset all the way to a cliff – and just caught the sunset in
time.

## Galle – a hot day in the old town

Thanks to the noise from the main road right next to us, we were up again
around 6 a.m., but a quick morning swim in the pool lifted the mood. For
under 50 cents per person, we took the bus the 30 km to **Galle**. We'd
barely arrived when people started approaching us from all sides for a tour
– not two metres went by without a "Hello Madam, tuk-tuk?" Since it was too
hot in the sun to walk, we even let ourselves get talked into a tour, which
was of course a letdown. Galle does have a beautiful old town, though: the
city was once an important trading port, and the Dutch-built,
UNESCO-protected fort even ensured that the old town survived the 2004
tsunami undamaged.

Since the midday sun and the noise were pretty unbearable, we skipped the
market and went looking for the (unmarked) bus stop. In the entire two
weeks, we never looked forward to the sea as much as we did that day. After
a brief "cooldown" in 29.6-degree water, the forecast rain never showed up,
and we grabbed a surfboard as well – which meant we'd definitely earned the
curry that followed. Shame we only discovered that really good curry on our
very last evening.

## The last day and the journey home

We let the last day start off relaxed by the pool and, after checking out,
headed to the beach one final time. One last surf session, then to finish it
off, a cold king coconut and a back massage – the perfect way to head into
the flight feeling relaxed. Well fed after one last curry, we set off toward
Negombo and, luckily, decided against the highway bus (which can take as
long as five hours) in favour of a relaxed 2.5-hour taxi ride. Shortly after
we left, the entire highway to Colombo was closed for three hours because
the Indian president was arriving – great timing on our part. In pouring
rain, which felt like fitting departure weather, we checked in for one last
short night; at 2 a.m. we headed to the airport. After about 17 hours of
travelling, we arrived back in a likewise sunny Germany.

## My takeaway

Sri Lanka is an incredibly diverse and varied island, with stunning
landscapes and impressive nature. The mountains especially, with their
waterfalls and tea plantations, are simply breathtaking. Personally, the
cities were far too loud for me – **Ella and Hiriketiya** were my favourites,
and it was really among fellow tourists that we felt most at ease. It's a
perfect country for surfing, since you can grab a board anywhere at any
time, and the food wasn't just cheap, it was seriously delicious – I could
easily have eaten like that for another two weeks.

My personal highlight was the **waterfall and the cooking class in Ella**.
The first sunrise hike with a view of Lion Rock was also an experience, and
the first surf lesson in the early morning, as well as releasing the baby
turtles, are things we won't forget anytime soon. A safari, on the other
hand, we can't recommend, and I won't be missing the constant haggling over
absolutely everything now that I'm back in Germany.

Two weeks is tight for a trip covering this much ground, but enough to take
in every impression, from the beach to the mountains.
