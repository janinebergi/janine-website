## A sleeping turtle
Hassan makes the hand signal for a turtle and then lets us know that it's currently asleep. And there it is: a huge turtle, roughly as long as I am, resting among some corals and sleeping. Even though I don't have a camera with me, this image burns itself into my brain. And for that alone the course was already worth it.

## Why get certified in Egypt

Back on our [last trip to Florida](/en/blog/florida-road-trip), Philipp – who already has a dive certification – was sad that all we could do on the Keys was snorkel. So for me it's clear: I urgently need to catch up. I would have loved to get certified right away, but in Germany it's simply twice as expensive and, because of the low temperatures, only possible from March to October. What would cost me almost 800 euros here costs me 320 euros in Egypt – and on the training dives I get to see far more than in Germany. Not that Germany doesn't have beautiful spots to offer, but most Open Water courses are done in a pool first and only the open-water dives take place in a lake. Why do that when you also have the option to dive in the Red Sea?

## Theory from the couch

After booking a resort in Egypt in January – one that's completely different from our usual holidays – I got straight in touch with the dive school at our resort. With PADI you can study the theory at home in advance, so you don't waste your holiday on tedious studying. So I'm looking forward to finally learning something new again and throw myself into the various lessons with great enthusiasm. In winter you end up sitting around at home far too much anyway, and I use that time to work through a theory session again and again. Highly motivated, I jot down everything I think is important – after all, your knowledge is tested not only at the end of each lesson but there's also a final exam at the end. But don't worry, I seem to remember that you can keep answering the questions until you pass, so there's basically no chance of failing – exactly the kind of exam I like.

## The first, less than relaxed dive

Finally, in May, the practical part can start too: the IDive Makadi Bay is right next to our hotel. Good thing I'd already booked and paid a deposit for the dive course, because my first time below the surface wasn't exactly relaxing. My instructor Hassan is still busy with someone who's just finishing their Open Water Diver, and my job is simply to wait underwater – not so easy after all. Breathing evenly from an air tank is hard for me, and so is regulating my buoyancy. Honestly, I'm just busy trying not to die. What on earth was that, I ask myself.

## First depth: 12 metres

Nervous, I wait for Philipp by the pool, honestly not particularly keen to start my next dive. This time Hassan only has time for me and the second dive starts much more relaxed: with 7 kg of weight around my belly, we go down to 12 m for the first time. Beforehand, out of the water, all the hand signals were explained to me, as well as my tasks and exactly how I'm supposed to handle them: clearing cramps, ditching weights in an emergency, switching between snorkel and regulator. I'm immediately reassured to have it explained to me what to do in emergency scenarios.

## The mask exercise

On the second dive already I reach the 18 m that are the goal of the Open Water Diver, and I learn to take off my mask underwater and put it back on fully flooded. I'll admit, swimming that stretch without a mask was definitely not my favourite task, but it was very helpful for learning how to clear a flooded mask underwater. At the time I had no idea how often I'd still need that – when you keep borrowing equipment, you unfortunately have to expect that it won't always fit.

## Between wind, navy and the house reef

Hassan makes an effort to ensure the dive isn't just about tasks, but that you also get to know the wonderful underwater world. We have a house reef where you can start a beautiful tour. Lucky for us, because we hit a week of very windy weather, and here it's the navy that decides whether boats are allowed out or not. As it turns out, no boats are allowed to set off for the rest of the week – so the pleasure of a boat dive is denied to me. But thankfully you can dive the house reef not only going left but also going right. In between we keep taking a rest day or do just one dive, hoping the navy will clear the boat trips again soon.

## Octopuses, moray eels and freezing feet

I'm fascinated by the underwater world, and I find Hassan's passion for it and his sharp eyes even more impressive: from the greatest distance he spots a well-camouflaged octopus that I'd never have noticed, even if I'd swum right over it. Moray eels and lionfish are a more common sight here, and the little bluespotted rays that hide so well on the sandy bottom I can now spot fairly well myself. The water is 24 degrees, but I freeze like crazy and by the end can barely suppress the shivering. Every time I come out of the water completely frozen through. Luckily Hassan takes pity on me and for the last dives I get a pair of neoprene socks – a real game changer. Apparently you lose an incredible amount of heat through your feet. Rather nice side effect: the fins stop chafing so badly, too.

## The last dive with Philipp

After I've also learned how to inflate a surface marker buoy and use a compass (which, admittedly, I'd already forgotten again by the time of my AOWD), Philipp gets to join me on my last dive – it's just the two of us, fantastic. Hassan has such a calm, easy manner, and thanks to the eye he has for the underwater world it feels like doing a completely different tour, even though you've already seen the reef a few times. Diving with a dive buddy you know is also much more relaxing as a beginner.

My highlight – besides the huge turtle I described at the start and the octopuses hiding in the corals – is a tiny little dragon fish, only a few centimetres long. In German it's probably a "Flügelrossfisch," with shimmering wing-like fins like a dragon.

After almost an hour underwater I surface, overjoyed and with a passed certification.

## Passed, and the first fun dive

We celebrate it the last and, at the same time, next day with my first fun dive – a dive on which I'm officially no longer a student. Since I didn't have my own underwater camera at that point, I sadly couldn't capture any of my first dives. All the nicer that on our last dive someone came along with a camera. So a few weeks after our holiday, at least I get sent a few pictures of these wonderful experiences.

The decision is made: I need my own action cam.
